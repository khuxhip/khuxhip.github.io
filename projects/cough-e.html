Cough-E

August 2025 – December 2025

Add a photo of the build here

A WALL-E inspired walking robot that mechanically synchronizes locomotion and cough drop dispensing through a single motor, designed to provide accessible relief to students living in university dorms.

Project Overview

For ME 370: Mechancial Design I, my team designed and manufactured COUGH-E, a walking robot that dispenses individually wrapped cough drops as it moves. The concept was created as to support students during flu seasons, providing a sanitary and approachable way to access symptom relief.

Both walking and dispensing systems were powered by a single motor. A compound gear train connected the linkage-driven legs to a dispenser. This allows the robot's forward motion to mechanically control when each cough drop was released.

Design Goals

COUGH-E was designed with the following requirements:

  • Walk without using powered wheels for locomotion.
  • Maintain a minimum forward speed of 4 m/min.
  • Dispense one individually wrapped cough drop approximately every 2 meters.
  • Keep the payload enclosed and avoid direct human contact.
  • Remain stable while walking and dispensing simultaneously.
  • Create a friendly appearance.

    • Design Process

    The initial dispenser used a horizontal revolver and slider-crank mechanism to open and close the release part. During prototyping, the slider consistently locked and the enclosed drum made refilling and maintenance more difficult.

    The second design replaced the initial with a vertically oriented revolver. While it dispensed more reliably, the gear reduction was too large to fit within the available chassis space as due to size constraints.

    Finally, we used a vertically stacked dispenser that released a cough drop every half-rotation, allowing to fit within the robot. However, the mechanism become more sensitive to differences in payload size and shape.

    PVA analysis was used to evaluate the linkage's foot path, determining the contact phase and estimating the stride legnth. The calculated duty cycle was about 35%, meaning each leg remained in contact with the ground for about 126 degrees of a complete rotation. These results help connect the walking motion to the dispenser by estimating how many shaft rotations were required for the robot to travel 2 meters. Calculated gear ratios were used to determined the required reduction between the legs and disepnsing mechanism.

    Results

    COUGH-E successfully demonstrated both walking and mechanically controlled dispensing. The robot achieved a walking speed of 4.35 m/min with a stride length of 4.375 cm. Although the dispensing system was predicted to dispense every 2.19 meters, the measured interval was 2.55 meters. This difference was likely caused by the added front supports, which allowed the robot to roll during the legs’ lift phase.
